# 🏛️ Deep Dive into Attic Greek Infinitives

## 🧠 1. Overview of the Infinitive in Attic Greek

The **infinitive** in Attic Greek is a **non-finite verbal form**, meaning it doesn’t indicate person or number. It often corresponds to the English “to [verb]” (e.g., **to speak**, **to go**), but its use is far broader and more complex in Greek. It functions both as a **verb** and a **noun**, participating in both verbal syntax and nominal functions.

---

## 🧱 2. Morphological Forms of the Infinitive

Each tense-stem (present, aorist, perfect, future) and voice (active, middle, passive) has its own infinitive form:

| Tense      | Active             | Middle             | Passive             |
|------------|--------------------|---------------------|----------------------|
| **Present**| λύειν               | λύεσθαι             | λύεσθαι              |
| **Aorist** | λῦσαι               | λύσασθαι            | λυθῆναι              |
| **Perfect**| λελυκέναι           | λελύσθαι            | λελύσθαι             |
| **Future** | λύσειν              | λύσεσθαι            | λυθήσεσθαι           |

**Notes**:
- **Present infinitives** describe **continuous** or **repeated** action.
- **Aorist infinitives** describe **simple** or **undefined** action.
- **Perfect infinitives** describe **completed** action with ongoing relevance.
- **Future infinitives** are rare; used mostly in indirect statements with verbs of saying or thinking when future time is implied.

---

## 🧠 3. Core Semantic Functions of the Infinitive

### 3.1 Aspectual Semantics

- **Present infinitive** → *durative*: emphasizes the process or repetition of an action  
  e.g. **φιλεῖν** = to be loving

- **Aorist infinitive** → *punctual*: views the action as a whole or a single event  
  e.g. **φιλῆσαι** = to love [once]

- **Perfect infinitive** → *stative/resultative*: indicates a completed action with lasting result  
  e.g. **πεφιληκέναι** = to have loved

- **Future infinitive** → *prospective*: refers to an action yet to happen  
  e.g. **φιλήσειν** = to be going to love

---

## 🧩 4. Syntactic Functions of the Infinitive

### 4.1 Subject of a Verb

> **To speak is easy.**  
> τὸ λέγειν ῥᾴδιον ἐστίν.

- The infinitive phrase functions as the **subject**.
- Often has the article (**τὸ**) to nominalize it.

---

### 4.2 Complement / Object of a Verb

> **He wants to speak.**  
> βούλεται λέγειν.

- The infinitive completes the meaning of the verb (βουλομαι, δύναμαι, ἄρχομαι, etc.).

---

### 4.3 Subject of Impersonal Expressions

> **It is necessary to flee.**  
> δεῖ φεύγειν.

- Common with impersonal verbs/expressions (δεῖ, χρή, ἔξεστιν, πρέπει, etc.).

---

### 4.4 Indirect Statement

> **He says that Socrates is wise.**  
> φησὶν Σωκράτη σοφὸν εἶναι.

- The infinitive introduces an indirect statement after verbs of **saying, thinking, believing, hearing, perceiving**.
- Subject is in **accusative**: Accusative + Infinitive construction (ACI).
- Predicate adjective/noun agrees with accusative subject.

---

### 4.5 Purpose Clause

> **He came to see the city.**  
> ἦλθεν ἰδεῖν τὴν πόλιν.

- Infinitive expresses **purpose**, often with motion verbs.
- Sometimes introduced with **ὡς** or **ἵνα**, or simply without any marker.

---

### 4.6 Explanatory or Epexegetical

> **He has the power to rule.**  
> ἔχει τὴν δύναμιν βασιλεύειν.

- Explains or specifies a previous noun, adjective, or verb.
- Often follows abstract nouns (δύναμις, ἐλπίς, βουλή, χρόνος, καιρός, etc.).

---

### 4.7 Result Clause

> **So as to make the city strong.**  
> ὥστε ἰσχυρὰν ποιῆσαι τὴν πόλιν.

- Introduced with **ὥστε** + infinitive.
- Implies a **natural** or **intended result**.

---

### 4.8 Commands / Exhortations (Rare)

> **Not to do this!**  
> μὴ ποιεῖν τοῦτο!

- Used in **prohibition**, often in **dramatic** or **indirect command** contexts.
- Usually with **μή** for negation.

---

### 4.9 Infinitive with Article (Articular Infinitive)

> **The to speak is easy.**  
> τὸ λέγειν ῥᾴδιον.

- Makes the infinitive function clearly as a **noun**.
- Can take **modifiers** (adverbs, objects), and **prepositional phrases**.

---

## 🧠 5. The Infinitive and Negation

| Context                                 | Negative Particle |
|-----------------------------------------|-------------------|
| General/Descriptive (factual)           | **οὐ**            |
| Prohibitive/Potential/Volitional        | **μή**            |

**Examples**:
- **οὐ λέγειν** – not to speak (as a fact)
- **μὴ λέγειν** – don’t speak (as a wish or command)

---

## 🌀 6. Noteworthy Syntactic Patterns

### 6.1 Double Accusative with Infinitive

> **I see Socrates drinking hemlock.**  
> ὁρῶ Σωκράτη κωνεῖν.

- Verbs of perception often take two accusatives: subject + object of infinitive.

---

### 6.2 Infinitive with Temporal Force

> **After he left, we came.**  
> μετὰ τὸ ἀπελθεῖν ἤλθομεν.

- Articular infinitive + preposition (μετά, πρό, ἐν, διά) can express time.

**Common combinations**:
- **πρὸ τοῦ + inf.** = before doing
- **μετὰ τὸ + inf.** = after doing
- **ἐν τῷ + inf.** = while doing
- **διά + τὸ + inf.** = because of doing

---

## 🔍 7. Infinitives in Special Constructions

### 7.1 Infinitive of Cause

> **He died because he drank.**  
> ἀπέθανεν διὰ τὸ πιεῖν.

- Used with **διά** + articular infinitive = causal clause.

---

### 7.2 Infinitive of Comparison

> **Rather than speak, he remained silent.**  
> μᾶλλον ἡσυχίαν ἦγεν ἢ λέγειν.

- Used in **comparative** constructions.

---

### 7.3 Potential Infinitive

> **He is likely to win.**  
> μέλλει νικᾶν.

- Often found with verbs of **intention, potentiality**, or **necessity**.

---

## 🏗️ 8. Infinitive as Center of Infinitive Clauses

Infinitive clauses are **non-finite clauses** where the infinitive governs other elements like a main verb would:

> **βούλομαι σε γράφειν τὴν ἐπιστολήν**  
> “I want *you to write the letter*.”

The infinitive can have:
- Its own **subject** (usually in accusative)
- Its own **object**
- **Adverbs**, **modifiers**, etc.

---

## 🔚 9. Conclusion

The infinitive in Attic Greek is extraordinarily versatile. It bridges the roles of **noun** and **verb**, fits into many **syntactic positions**, and conveys nuanced meanings across **aspect**, **modality**, and **purpose**.

# 📚 15 Examples of Infinitives in Attic Greek

Each example below includes:
- ✅ The Greek sentence
- 🔍 Translation
- 📌 Explanation of the infinitive's use

---

### 1. **Subject of a Verb**

**Greek**: τὸ λέγειν ἐστὶν ἡδύ.  
**Translation**: *To speak is pleasant.*  
**Explanation**: The infinitive **λέγειν** functions as the **subject** of the verb **ἐστίν**, nominalized by the article **τὸ**.

---

### 2. **Complement of a Verb**

**Greek**: βούλομαι μαθεῖν τὴν ἀλήθειαν.  
**Translation**: *I wish to learn the truth.*  
**Explanation**: The infinitive **μαθεῖν** completes the verb **βούλομαι** (I wish), functioning as a **complementary infinitive**.

---

### 3. **Subject of an Impersonal Expression**

**Greek**: δεῖ τοὺς ἀνδρείους μάχεσθαι.  
**Translation**: *The brave must fight.*  
**Explanation**: The infinitive **μάχεσθαι** is the subject of the impersonal verb **δεῖ** (it is necessary).

---

### 4. **Accusative + Infinitive (Indirect Statement)**

**Greek**: φησὶν Σωκράτη σοφὸν εἶναι.  
**Translation**: *He says that Socrates is wise.*  
**Explanation**: **εἶναι** introduces an indirect statement; **Σωκράτη** is the subject in the accusative case.

---

### 5. **Purpose Clause**

**Greek**: ἦλθεν ἵνα ἴδῃ τὸν φίλον.  
**Translation**: *He came to see his friend.*  
**Explanation**: The infinitive **ἴδῃ** (aorist subjunctive in this case), often replaced by an infinitive: **ἦλθεν ἰδεῖν**. Purpose expressed by the infinitive after a motion verb.

---

### 6. **Result Clause**

**Greek**: οὕτως ἔπραξεν ὥστε πάντας θαυμάζειν.  
**Translation**: *He acted in such a way that everyone admired him.*  
**Explanation**: **θαυμάζειν** is the infinitive of result introduced by **ὥστε**.

---

### 7. **Explanatory / Epexegetical Infinitive**

**Greek**: ἔχει τὴν δύναμιν νικᾶν τοὺς πολεμίους.  
**Translation**: *He has the power to defeat the enemies.*  
**Explanation**: The infinitive **νικᾶν** explains what kind of power is meant—**epexegetical** to **δύναμιν**.

---

### 8. **Infinitive with Article as Noun**

**Greek**: τὸ φυλάττειν τοὺς νόμους σώζει τὴν πόλιν.  
**Translation**: *Obeying the laws saves the city.*  
**Explanation**: The infinitive **φυλάττειν**, with article **τὸ**, functions as a full noun phrase.

---

### 9. **Temporal Infinitive**

**Greek**: μετὰ τὸ νικῆσαι τοὺς πολεμίους, ἐθυσίασαν.  
**Translation**: *After defeating the enemies, they offered a sacrifice.*  
**Explanation**: **μετὰ τὸ νικῆσαι** expresses **time** (after doing something).

---

### 10. **Cause / Causal Infinitive**

**Greek**: ἀπέθανεν διὰ τὸ ὑβρίσαι.  
**Translation**: *He died because he acted arrogantly.*  
**Explanation**: The phrase **διὰ τὸ ὑβρίσαι** gives the **reason**—causal infinitive.

---

### 11. **Potential Infinitive**

**Greek**: μέλλει πεσεῖν.  
**Translation**: *He is about to fall.*  
**Explanation**: **πεσεῖν** is a **future infinitive** used with **μέλλει** to express **potential/future action**.

---

### 12. **Infinitive with Perception Verb**

**Greek**: ἤκουσα αὐτὸν λέγοντα τὴν ἀλήθειαν.  
**Translation**: *I heard him speaking the truth.*  
**Explanation**: Double accusative (**αὐτόν** + **λέγοντα**) shows **accusative + infinitive/perception**.

---

### 13. **Negative Infinitive with οὐ**

**Greek**: ἐνόμισαν οὐ φεύγειν τοὺς πολεμίους.  
**Translation**: *They thought the enemies were not fleeing.*  
**Explanation**: **οὐ** negates a **factual/indicative** infinitive in indirect speech.

---

### 14. **Negative Infinitive with μή**

**Greek**: ἐκέλευσεν μὴ λέγειν περαιτέρω.  
**Translation**: *He ordered (them) not to speak further.*  
**Explanation**: **μή** used for **volitional/prohibitive** infinitive.

---

### 15. **Comparative Infinitive**

**Greek**: κρεῖττον ἐστι σιγᾶν ἢ ψεύδεσθαι.  
**Translation**: *It is better to be silent than to lie.*  
**Explanation**: **σιγᾶν** and **ψεύδεσθαι** are compared using **ἤ**; infinitives used in **comparative construction**.

---

## 📚 More Examples of Infinitives in Attic Greek (Examples 16–30)

---

### 16. **Infinitive with Adjective (Verbal Adjective + Infinitive)**

**Greek**: ῥᾴδιόν ἐστι νικᾶν τοὺς ἀπείρους.  
**Translation**: *It is easy to defeat the inexperienced.*  
**Explanation**: **νικᾶν** is governed by the adjective **ῥᾴδιον**, forming an adjective + infinitive construction.

---

### 17. **Infinitive of Intention**

**Greek**: ἐξῆλθον μάχεσθαι.  
**Translation**: *They went out to fight.*  
**Explanation**: Infinitive **μάχεσθαι** expresses **intended action** following a verb of motion.

---

### 18. **Infinitive in Indirect Question**

**Greek**: οὐκ ᾔδει τί ποιεῖν.  
**Translation**: *He did not know what to do.*  
**Explanation**: **ποιεῖν** is used in an **indirect question** introduced by **τί**.

---

### 19. **Infinitive after Comparative + ἤ**

**Greek**: βέλτιον ἐστὶν ἀποθανεῖν ἢ δουλεύειν.  
**Translation**: *It is better to die than to be a slave.*  
**Explanation**: Infinitives **ἀποθανεῖν** and **δουλεύειν** are compared using **ἤ**.

---

### 20. **Articular Infinitive with ἐν + Temporal Force**

**Greek**: ἐν τῷ γράφειν, ἦλθεν ὁ φίλος.  
**Translation**: *While he was writing, his friend came.*  
**Explanation**: **ἐν τῷ γράφειν** shows **contemporaneous time** using the **articular infinitive**.

---

### 21. **Infinitive with Preposition (διά + cause)**

**Greek**: διὰ τὸ φιλεῖν τὸν πατέρα, ἔδρασεν.  
**Translation**: *He acted because he loved his father.*  
**Explanation**: The **causal infinitive** is introduced by **διά + article + infinitive**.

---

### 22. **Infinitive with μέλλω + Future Action**

**Greek**: μέλλομεν καταλύσειν τὴν πόλιν.  
**Translation**: *We are about to overthrow the city.*  
**Explanation**: **Future infinitive** used after **μέλλω** to indicate **future intent**.

---

### 23. **Infinitive with Personal Subject (Double Accusative)**

**Greek**: κελεύει τοὺς ἄνδρας στρατεύεσθαι.  
**Translation**: *He orders the men to march.*  
**Explanation**: The **infinitive clause** has its own subject (**τοὺς ἄνδρας**) in the accusative.

---

### 24. **Infinitive in Indirect Statement with δοκεῖ**

**Greek**: δοκεῖ αὐτὸν σοφὸν εἶναι.  
**Translation**: *He seems to be wise.*  
**Explanation**: The **infinitive εἶναι** forms an **indirect statement** governed by the impersonal verb **δοκεῖ**.

---

### 25. **Prohibition with Infinitive**

**Greek**: φεῦ, μὴ πράττειν ταῦτα!  
**Translation**: *Alas, do not do these things!*  
**Explanation**: **μή** negates an **infinitive of prohibition**, often seen in tragedy or commands.

---

### 26. **Infinitive after ἔξεστι(ν)**

**Greek**: ἔξεστιν ἡμῖν παίζειν.  
**Translation**: *We are allowed to play.*  
**Explanation**: Infinitive **παίζειν** completes the impersonal **ἔξεστιν**, expressing **permission**.

---

### 27. **Infinitive of Fear (after verbs of fearing)**

**Greek**: φοβοῦμαι μὴ ἔλθειν ὁ ἐχθρός.  
**Translation**: *I fear that the enemy may come.*  
**Explanation**: Classical Greek prefers **subjunctive**, but infinitive sometimes appears in later Greek or freer constructions.

---

### 28. **Infinitive as Predicate Noun**

**Greek**: εἶναι φιλόσοφος καλόν.  
**Translation**: *To be a philosopher is noble.*  
**Explanation**: The infinitive **εἶναι φιλόσοφος** acts as a **predicate noun**.

---

### 29. **Infinitive with Abstract Subject (τὸ καλόν)**

**Greek**: τὸ καλὸν ἐστὶ τὸ βοηθεῖν τοῖς φίλοις.  
**Translation**: *The noble thing is helping friends.*  
**Explanation**: The infinitive **βοηθεῖν** is nominalized and forms the **subject** of a judgment about value.

---

### 30. **Infinitive with φημί in Indirect Statement**

**Greek**: φησὶ τὸν Ἀριστοτέλην σοφώτατον εἶναι.  
**Translation**: *He says that Aristotle is very wise.*  
**Explanation**: ACI construction with **εἶναι** and **accusative subject**, standard indirect speech after **φημί**.
